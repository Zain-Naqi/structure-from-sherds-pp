Literature Review:

Geometric Feature Matching Approaches
Initial computational pipelines attempt to solve 3D reassembly by relying heavily on geometric feature matching and extracting distinct curvature and roughness descriptors from fractured surfaces to guide pairwise alignment. While these classical methods proved highly effective for assembling generic solids with rich and irregular break topologies, they struggle significantly in the archaeological domain. Ceramic pottery typically shatters into fragments which are extremely thin and have sparsely featured fracture surfaces. When geometric methods rely solely on matching these ambiguous boundaries, they often generate many indistinguishable false-positive matches, causing sequential assemblies to fail due to error propagation.  Hence, researchers in the domain noted that scalable and globally optimal assembling of fragmented symmetric objects is still a critical open challenge. 


Data Driven and Deep Learning Approaches
Recent advancements in the field have leveraged deep learning to automate feature extraction and global alignment. Learning-based models like PotNet use 1D convolutional networks to predict the placement of a piece, while end-to-end architectures like Jigsaw, FRAISER, and PuzzleFusion++ jointly learn segmentation, feature matching, and alignment using attention mechanisms, transformers, and diffusion models. While these approaches achieve impressive reconstruction accuracy on synthetic datasets like Breaking Bad, they show severe limitations when evaluated on real world archaeological pottery. Real ceramic sherds lack the distinctive bulky fracture topologies found in synthetic objects, rendering deep features unreliable and making data-driven models highly susceptible to false positive matches and poor generalisation.


Parallel Search Approaches
To address the challenges introduced by feature-sparse pottery, recent methods have utilised geometric priors, such as the pots’ axis of symmetry, to limit possible fragment placements while employing parallel search strategies to efficiently explore candidate alignments. Hong et al. introduced Structure-from-Sherds (SfS), which relies on identifying a base fragment to guide the incremental reconstruction. Structure-from-Sherds++ (SfS++) improved upon this using a parallel search algorithm named base-agnostic multigraph beam search (BAISER) to explore multiple assembly paths simultaneously. While SfS++ represents the current state of the art on real world axially symmetric pottery, the beam search strategy faces scaling challenges as the fragment count increases.. For highly fragmented pots, distinguishing between true and false configurations is extremely difficult in the early stages of assembly. To overcome these challenges, SfS++ must utilise a big search tree with very broad search branches creating a tradeoff between reconstruction accuracy and exponential computational runtime. Consequently, the struggle to maintain flawless performance across large pots remains.


Genetic Algorithms for Reconstruction
Genetic algorithms have proved to be highly successful in 2D reconstruction tasks, showing that they can navigate massive combinatorial spaces without committing early to greedy decisions. Sholomon et al. presents a GA-based solver for extremely large jigsaw puzzles, effectively handling unknown dimensions, pieces, and orientations using a specialized crossover procedure to merge correct sub-assemblies. Expanding to archaeological contexts, Sizikova and Funkhouser applied GAs to global assembly of fragmented 2D wall paintings suffering from severe erosions, utilising fitness functions that reward dense match connections. These works highlight the scalability and strength of genetic algorithms in solving ambiguous 2D assembly problems. 
To our knowledge, direct application of genetic algorithms on 3D pottery reassembly remains largely unexplored. While some efforts introduce evolutionary concepts to 3D artefact restoration, their underlying logic differs entirely from geometric reassembly. For example, Kashihara proposed a GA that optimises 3D fragment positions by projecting them on 2D, mapping silhouettes and ORB image features with known “correct” reference images. Because this approach fundamentally requires prior knowledge of the target shape’s 2D projections to evaluate fitness, it cannot scale to the blind reassembly of unknown 3D pottery. Therefore, developing population-based evolutionary search which operates directly on the 3D geometric fracture constraints remains an open challenge that we tackle in this paper.
